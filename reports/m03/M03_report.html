Summary. Pakistan's weekly Sensitive Price Indicator (SPI) is reported as one national figure, but PBS publishes prices for 51 items in 17 cities. Milestone 02 built a 23,409-row city-item-week dataset and found that city food price premiums are negatively rank-correlated with district deprivation. Milestone 03 asks whether the same data can give early warning (SDG 2.c): using only what is known in week t, can we flag the city-item prices that will move abnormally by week t+1? Abnormal means a change above the item's own 90th percentile of non-zero changes, fitted on training weeks only. On 7,095 labelled rows (205 abnormal, 2.9%) we compare two naive benchmarks with logistic regression, a decision tree and KNN. We use a time-ordered split and forward-chaining cross-validation that never uses a label from the test period. On the four held-out weeks, logistic regression reaches ROC-AUC 0.692 [0.636, 0.746] (row-level bootstrap), and it is above 0.5 in each test week. We reject H3's null for these weeks, while noting that four weeks cannot establish a general result. A one-line rule that scores each item by its category's past abnormal rate does as well (0.708 [0.659, 0.753]), and it is also the cheapest alerting policy in our cost analysis. Precision stays near 5%. The models are at most a triage aid, not an automatic trigger. In these test weeks, alerts pay off only if a missed shock is judged to cost about 20 or more false alarms.

2. Data ingestion, cleaning and structural readiness

2.1 Collection audit

Each PBS weekly release page (/weekly-sensitive-price-indicator-spi-for-the-week-ended-on-DD-MM-YYYY/) links an Annexure workbook. A rate-limited script tried the Thursday, Wednesday and Friday page for each of 73 weeks from 2025-05-15 and logged every request, URL and SHA-256 hash. It found release pages for 12 weeks, most of them weeks already retrieved for Milestone 01 (the re-downloads were byte-identical). After dropping 12 duplicate files, the panel holds 27 weeks. Other weeks may exist under URLs we did not try, so we do not treat the archive as complete. The week after our freeze date (2026-10-01) was stored, unopened, as a holdout. External files were downloaded from pbs.gov.pk and are kept unchanged with their URLs and hashes.

Validation. The parser asserts, for every week: 3 blocks, 17 cities, 51 items, 867 rows; no duplicate key; MIN ≤ AVG ≤ MAX; and zeros only as all-zero triples. Against PBS's own national columns, the cross-city minimum and maximum match PBS's national MIN and MAX in 1,377 of 1,377 item-weeks. The geometric mean of the city averages matches PBS's national average within 0.1% in 1,377 of 1,377. The arithmetic mean misses by a median of 0.42%. We therefore use the geometric mean as the reference price.

2.2 Reshaping and alignment

The raw sheet (179 rows × 25 columns) is not tidy. City names are column headers, each spanning MIN/AVG/MAX cells, and three tables are stacked in one sheet. We melt each block into one row per city × item, concatenate the blocks and weeks, and move PBS's national columns into a separate validation table. We then join the item classification (on item_id), the city attributes (on city) and the CPI (on the month of week_end). Every join is many-to-one, and the row count is asserted unchanged (Table 2).

Key alignment decisions.

  • Karachi: one SPI market, but six PSLM districts and seven census districts. PSLM values are population-weighted.
  • Islamabad and Rawalpindi: one contiguous market, kept as two administrative units.
  • District totals: PSLM has no urban sample for Khuzdar and publishes FIES only for district totals, so all indicators use district totals. These are deprivation measures, not income.

2.3 Cleaning and missing data

Nothing is deleted. Zero MIN/AVG/MAX triples (83 rows, 0.35%) are set to missing and flagged: rice IRRI-6/9 is never quoted in Gujranwala, Lahore or Sialkot, and Khuzdar has no gas quote in two weeks. PBS itself excludes these cities from its national mean. Missing counts and percentages for every column are in Appendix Table A1 of the Milestone 02 report. Most of the missingness is structural: weekly change columns are undefined across non-consecutive weeks (44.3% of rows), because only 15 pairs of weeks are 6-8 days apart. Table 3 gives the choice made for each kind of missingness. Selection bias: we exclude structural non-quotes from price comparisons, so results describe quoting markets and may still reflect selection bias, because a missing quote does not prove an item is unavailable in that city. The gaps in weekly coverage also mean the volatility analysis sees only the weeks we could retrieve.

2.4 Transformations, scaling and encoding

Log transformation. Prices are compared in logs because premiums are multiplicative: a 10% premium means the same thing for salt and for a 20 kg flour bag. Within an item, the median skewness of prices falls from 0.22 (raw) to 0.02 (log). Prices pooled across items in PKR are not a meaningful distribution (skewness 2.13), because they mix units. The main variable is rel_price = log(city price / national geometric mean), which sums to zero across cities in each item-week. District urban population is strongly right-skewed (skewness 2.30, driven by Karachi). Its log (log_pop_urban) has skewness -0.72. Scaling. The four deprivation indicators are min-max scaled to 0-1 (higher = more deprived) and averaged into deprivation_composite. Prices are z-scored within each item and week (price_z_item_week). All constants come from the training weeks only. Encoding. Nominal strings become 0/1 dummy flags with fixed reference levels: Punjab for province, storable staple for item category (Table 4). The model-ready file data/processed/m02/model_ready_food.parquet (14,607 food rows × 44 columns) carries 7 dummy columns. Cities will enter H1 with sum-to-zero coding, so each coefficient is a premium relative to the average city.

3.2 City food price premiums

For each city we average rel_price over weeks for each food item. The item-level means are the observations, so n is 31-32 items, not thousands of rows. Islamabad's mean premium is +14.1% (95% t-interval [8.9, 19.6]), followed by Rawalpindi +10.2% and Karachi +5.2%. Sukkur (-7.1%) and Bannu (-7.4%) are lowest (Fig. 1). 8 of 17 cities differ from zero at α = 0.05. After a Bonferroni correction for 17 tests, 4 remain: Islamabad, Rawalpindi, Sukkur, Bannu. Premiums persist: of 3,269 consecutive-week pairs in which a city was above the cross-city median, 92.7% stayed above it the next week. Restricting to pairs where the price actually changed, the figure is 84.1%.

3.3 Dispersion by food category

The coefficient of variation (CV) puts items priced in different units on one scale. Mean cross-city CV is highest for perishables (0.157, 11 items) and prepared food (0.144), lower for storable staples (0.107, 10 items), and lowest for branded packaged goods (0.026), which carry printed or distributor prices (Fig. 2). A one-tailed Welch comparison of perishables against storables gives p = 0.037, and a rank-based check gives p = 0.057. With about ten items per group this is suggestive, not conclusive. Categories were assigned on storability and pricing mechanism before any dispersion was computed.

3.4 Outlier diagnostic

We apply both rules taught in the course to rel_price and to weekly log changes, each at two tiers (Table 6, Fig. 3). Masking and variance inflation: the two rules disagree sharply. On rel_price, the IQR is narrow because many branded items are priced identically everywhere, so the quartile fences sit close and flag 3,649 values. The SD, by contrast, is inflated by the extreme values themselves, which pulls the 3-SD fence outward and masks them: only 434 are flagged. On weekly changes the IQR is exactly 0, so the quartile rule flags every non-zero change and is unusable. We checked the eight most extreme relative prices against the raw workbooks. 7 equal PBS's own national minimum or maximum for that item-week, and the other is a genuine printed quote (Sukkur tomatoes, PKR 20 per 1 kg). They are therefore true prices: we keep and flag them rather than trim them, because large moves are what H3 studies.

4. Hypotheses and analytical plan

Each hypothesis is mapped to master-dataset columns in Table 9. Targets marked † are derived from existing columns, with the rule for each fixed in Milestone 02. Milestone 03 builds the final H3 label (Section 5) and tests H3 (Sections 6-8); the base rate and row count quoted for H3 below are the whole-period description from Milestone 02, which the models never use.

  • H1, persistent premiums (SDG 2.1). (a) In log pc,i,t = αi,t + βc + ε with Σβc = 0: H0: βc = 0 for every city c; H1: βc ≠ 0 for at least one c. (b) H0: P(above median at t+1 | above at t) = 0.5; H1: > 0.5, tested on consecutive weeks and repeated for changed prices only. If some cities persistently pay more for the same basket, access to food differs by place in a way the national figure hides.
  • H2, perishability and dispersion (SDG 2.c). H0: μCV, perishable = μCV, storable; H1: μCV, perishable > μCV, storable. Greater dispersion in perishables points to market-functioning frictions.
  • H3, abnormal next-week movement (SDG 2.c, early warning). H0: a classifier on lagged features achieves AUC = 0.5 on a time-ordered test set; H1: AUC > 0.5. "Abnormal" means a change larger than the item's own 90th percentile of non-zero changes in the training weeks. We use non-zero changes because 75% of weekly food price changes are zero, and the plain 90th percentile would be 0 for 13 of the 32 food items. Base rate: 2.9%.
  • H4, representation and model fairness (SDG 10). H0: model error rates and data quality (share of MIN = MAX records) are equal across city groups (province, population tercile, deprivation tercile); H1: at least one group differs.
  • H5, burden versus price (SDG 2.1, SDG 10). H0: Spearman ρ(city premium, deprivation) = 0; H1: ρ ≠ 0, two-sided, n = 17. EDA already points to ρ < 0 (§3.5). The test will be repeated under both deprivation definitions.

5. Baseline models and justification

5.1 Prediction task, label and features

Unit and target. One row is one city × food item × week t. The target y = 1 if the item's price in that city moves abnormally between week t and the next consecutive release t+1. "Abnormal" means |Δlog price| above the item's own 90th percentile of non-zero weekly changes, because the plain 90th percentile is 0 for 13 of the 32 food items. Items with fewer than five non-zero changes in the training rows get no threshold and are dropped (5 items in the final split). The positive class is the abnormal move, the event a price monitor wants to catch. Thresholds are fitted on training rows only, separately in every fold and in the final split. If they were fitted on all weeks, the test weeks would help define what counts as abnormal.

Why only 15 of 27 weeks carry a label. A label needs the next week's price, and only 15 weeks are followed by a release 6-8 days later. This is a gap in PBS's published weekly archive, not in our method. Described with whole-period thresholds, the labelled data has 7,095 rows (473 per week), of which 205 are abnormal (2.9%). The models never see these whole-period thresholds.

Features. Every feature is known at week t (Table 10). A change that cannot be computed because the previous week was not published (40% of rows for this week's change, 73% for last week's) is set to 0 and flagged with a has_ indicator. The model can then tell "no change" from "not observed" (the Milestone 02 rule: flag, don't impute). We deliberately do not add one dummy per item: with about 200 positives this would overfit, and it would swamp KNN's distances.

5.2 Benchmarks and models

Table 11 lists the five approaches. The two benchmarks set the floor. The majority class shows why accuracy is misleading here. The category base-rate rule encodes the domain view that perishables move more, which Milestone 02 supported (H2). It is a tough baseline: it needs no fitting beyond four rates, and any model that cannot beat it is not worth deploying. The three course models represent three different ideas: a linear probability model, axis-aligned rules and similarity. Logistic regression and the tree use class_weight="balanced", which weights each positive row by n/(2 × positives) and each negative by n/(2 × negatives), so the rare class counts as much as the common one. KNN has no class weights; its min-max scaler, like the logistic model's z-score scaler, sits inside an sklearn Pipeline, so it is refitted on the training rows of each fold.

6. Experimental setup

6.1 Time-ordered split, purge and stratification

We train on the first 11 labelled weeks (2025-07-17 to 2026-03-26) and test on the last 4 (2026-04-02, 2026-06-04, 2026-08-13, 2026-08-20): 73% / 27% by weeks. Purge rule: a row at week t is labelled from week t+1, so a training row whose t+1 is an evaluation week is dropped. Here the last training week (2026-03-26) is followed by the first test week, so its 541 candidate rows are purged, and the final models learn from 10 weeks. After purging and labelling, the training set has 4,560 rows (135 abnormal, 2.96%) and the test set has 1,824 rows (54 abnormal, 2.96%): 71% / 29% by rows. The two base rates are nearly equal, so the test weeks are not unusually calm or volatile. The fitted sample (6,384 rows) is smaller than the 7,095-row description in §5.1 for two reasons: the purged week is removed, and thresholds fitted on training rows alone leave 5 items without a label, against 4 under whole-period thresholds.

Why not a random stratified split? The brief's example is a random stratified split. Our rows are a panel: the same 17 × 28 city-item series recur every week, and neighbouring weeks share shocks. A random split would train on later weeks to predict earlier ones, which PBS could never do in practice. It also lets the model learn week-specific shocks that appear on both sides of the split. We keep time order. The purpose of stratification, keeping the rare class represented in every part, is met differently: every validation fold and the test set are checked to contain positives (16-28 per validation week; 54 in test), and logistic regression and the tree use balanced class weights. Class weights change what the model optimises; they are not a substitute for stratification, which is why the positive counts are checked. §6.4 compares random and time-ordered folds.

6.2 Forward-chaining cross-validation

Outer boundary. Cross-validation may only use labels that end before the test period. The last training week (2026-03-26) is labelled from the first test week, so it is excluded from CV as well as from final training. This leaves 10 development weeks (to 2025-12-18). An earlier version of our code used that week as a CV validation week, which let test-period prices shape its labels; an independent review caught this, and every result here comes from the corrected design. The code now asserts that every CV label, in training and validation, ends before the first test week. Folds. Within the development weeks we use an expanding window (Fig. 6). Fold 1 trains on labelled weeks 1-5 and validates on week 6. Fold 2 trains on weeks 1-6 and validates on week 7, and so on, giving 5 folds. Each fold rebuilds its own label thresholds, scaler and model from its training weeks and applies the purge rule (4 folds lose their last training week to it). Each validation week holds only 16-28 abnormal moves, so fold-to-fold variance is large. We report the mean ± SD across folds and every fold's AUC (Appendix Table A1).

6.3 Hyperparameter tuning

Each grid in Table 11 runs inside the five folds; no test-period information enters this step. Settings are compared by mean validation ROC-AUC, which does not depend on a threshold and is not fooled by the majority class. When several settings are within one fold-SD of the best, we take the simplest: smallest C, smallest depth with the largest leaves, or largest K. With so few positives per fold, the difference between near-equal settings is mostly noise (Fig. 7). Logistic regression: C hardly matters. C = 0.01, the strongest regularisation, has the best mean (0.641 ± 0.102). Decision tree: depth 4 had the best mean (0.673 ± 0.052), but depth 2 is within one SD, so we keep the simpler tree (0.641 ± 0.117); a depth-2 tree is also four readable rules. KNN: AUC rises steadily with K and peaks at the grid's edge (n_neighbors=201, weights=distance, metric=manhattan, features=all; 0.657 ± 0.128). Using the four continuous features alone is clearly worse (best 0.607), so the category dummies carry most of what KNN finds.

6.4 Leakage checks

Random folds are over-optimistic. For the chosen logistic model, ordinary shuffled StratifiedKFold (5 folds) on the training rows gives AUC 0.710 ± 0.038, against 0.641 for forward chaining: a gap of 0.070. This is a comparison, not a controlled measurement of leakage: the two schemes also differ in training size, and the random folds reuse labels fitted on all training rows. But it points the way we expected: ignoring time order makes the model look better than it is. Shuffled-label sanity check. Refitting the final logistic model on permuted training labels and scoring the real test labels gives AUC 0.583 for the seed-42 shuffle. Over 200 shuffles the mean is 0.503 (5th-95th percentile 0.374-0.626), and 0 of 200 reached the real test AUC. This is consistent with the pipeline behaving correctly, but it is a sanity check, not proof that nothing leaks (it could not have detected the CV boundary problem above). It is also not a formal test of H3, because rows in the same week are not exchangeable and tuning is not repeated under the null. Asserts in the code: no week after the cutoff is read; no evaluation week is in any training set; every CV label ends before the test period; thresholds come from training rows; the purge is applied; the mean shuffled AUC is within 0.03 of 0.5.

H3 decision. For logistic regression and the decision tree, the lower bound of the row-level bootstrap 95% CI for test ROC-AUC is above 0.5 (logistic 0.692 [0.636, 0.746]; tree 0.610 [0.576, 0.650]); for KNN it is not (0.561 [0.487, 0.636]). The bootstrap treats rows as independent, but rows in the same week share shocks, so these intervals are too narrow. As a check that respects weeks, we score each test week separately (Appendix Table A3): logistic regression is above 0.5 in all 4 weeks (range 0.542-0.748), the category rule in 3. We therefore reject H0 for logistic regression in these test weeks: information available at week t ranked next-week abnormal moves better than chance. With only four weeks this is evidence of a modest signal, not an established general result; the Week 14 holdout is the next test. Test PR-AUC is 0.052, under twice the base rate.

Does any ML model beat the simple rule? In cross-validation, logistic regression is ahead of the category rule on average (0.641 ± 0.102 vs 0.617 ± 0.107), but only in 3 of 5 folds. On the test weeks, the category rule is slightly ahead (0.708 vs 0.692). The paired bootstrap difference (logistic minus rule) is -0.015, 95% CI [-0.074, 0.041], so we cannot tell them apart. The depth-2 tree scores below the rule on test (row-bootstrap difference CI [-0.154, -0.036], with the same independence caveat). KNN had the highest CV mean but also the largest SD, and it fell to 0.561 on test. The honest reading is that most of the usable signal is "what kind of food is it". The ablation agrees. Logistic regression with only the three category dummies scores 0.617 in CV and 0.708 on test. The full feature set adds about 0.02 AUC in CV and nothing on test.

Accuracy misleads. The majority class is 97.0% accurate and catches nothing. The category rule never reaches a score of 0.5 and KNN does so for only 4 rows, so at that threshold they flag (almost) nothing, and their accuracy-to-F1 rows look like the majority class. Balanced class weights push logistic regression the other way: at 0.5 it flags 813 rows, with recall 76% but precision 5.0%. 0.5 is an arbitrary cut-off. §8 chooses thresholds from costs.

Why KNN struggles (Unit 11 limitations). With about 3% positives, most neighbourhoods of 5-25 rows contain no abnormal move at all, so the vote is 0 almost everywhere. Binary dummies count as much in the distance as real price changes, and irrelevant features add noise. Many rows are identical (no change, not observed), which produces ties. Only very large neighbourhoods give a usable score, and then KNN is a smoothed local base rate, which explains its peak at the edge of the K grid.

Label sensitivity. Under the Milestone 02 "contract" rule (plain 90th percentile; any change counts for 13 items) the test base rate is 7.2%. Logistic regression reaches 0.585 and the category rule 0.523, so the task is harder. The refined label is the one we defend.

8. Asymmetric error costs and confusion-matrix diagnostics

8.1 Who bears each error

The alert is meant for a PBS analyst, or a district Price Control Committee or price magistrate deciding where to send inspectors or which items to watch next week.

  • A false negative (missed abnormal move) is, at its most harmful, a jump in tomatoes, onions, flour or ghee that nobody was warned about (abnormal moves also include sharp falls, which matter to producers and traders but less to consumers). No early stock release, inspection or public notice happens, and households absorb the shock. Households in more deprived districts spend a larger share of income on food, so the harm falls hardest on those least able to absorb it (H5).
  • A false positive (false alarm) is an inspection visit or watch-list entry that was not needed. It costs staff time and may cause friction with traders. Repeated false alarms cause alert fatigue: if most alerts are wrong, people stop trusting the system.

Cost matrix (Provost and Fawcett, Ch. 7). cost(TP) = cost(TN) = 0, cost(FP) = 1 unit and cost(FN) = r units. We put the cost in one cell only, to avoid the double counting Provost and Fawcett warn about. We do not also credit a "benefit" for true positives. Flagging a row with probability p of being abnormal is worth it when p·r > 1 − p, i.e. p > 1/(1 + r). For an average row, p is the training base rate (2.96%), so "flag everything" beats "flag nothing" exactly when r > (1 − p)/p = 32.8, close to the inverse base rate (33.8). r is a value judgment made by people, not something the data decides, so we vary it over {1, 5, 10, 20, 35}. For each r and model, the threshold that minimises total cost is chosen on the pooled out-of-fold CV predictions (all from development weeks) and then applied to the test predictions.

9. Drivers and initial policy insights

Logistic regression. Inputs are z-scored inside the pipeline, so each coefficient is the change in log-odds per one standard deviation, and ecoef is the odds ratio (OR) per SD (Table 18, Fig. 11). For a 0/1 feature, one SD is a fixed fraction of the switch from 0 to 1, so these ORs rank features rather than describe a full category change. They are associations, not causes. The top five:

  • cat_branded_packaged (OR 0.60): one SD more of being a branded/packaged item is associated with 40% lower odds of an abnormal move next week.
  • price_changed (OR 1.47): one SD more of a price change this week is associated with about 1.47 times the odds of an abnormal move next week.
  • cat_perishable (OR 1.33): one SD more of being a perishable is associated with about 1.33 times the odds of an abnormal move next week.
  • has_dlog (OR 0.79): one SD more of having an observed change this week (previous week published) is associated with 21% lower odds of an abnormal move next week.
  • prov_ICT (OR 0.80): one SD more of being in Islamabad is associated with 20% lower odds of an abnormal move next week.
abs_dlog gets a small coefficient because it is correlated with price_changed (r = 0.50 in the training rows), and the two share the credit (Unit 09). The has_ flags are negative mostly because they mark which weeks had a published previous week, so they partly stand in for time period, not for a market effect.

Decision tree. The depth-2 tree splits first on branded/packaged, then on this week's change for other items, and on relative price for branded ones; the diagram is in the notebook (Part D.2) and reports/m03/figures/fig21_tree.png. Its impurity importance is 0.58 for cat_branded_packaged, 0.29 for abs_dlog and 0.12 for rel_price. Its leaves read as rules (Table 19). The top rule is: IF the item is not branded/packaged AND its price moved by more than about 1% this week, THEN the training abnormal rate is 6.9%, against 3.0% overall. Branded items whose price is at or above the national reference had no abnormal move in training. Worked Gini (root). Unweighted, the root holds 135 positives in 4,560 rows (Gini 0.0575); the split sends 130/3,540 left and 5/1,020 right, gain 0.0004. With balanced weights (positive 16.9, negative 0.515), the root is exactly 50/50 (Gini 0.50), the children have Gini 0.494 and 0.239, and the weighted gain is 0.040, the largest available.

Cross-model check. Both models put the branded/packaged flag first, and both give recent price movement a top role (price_changed in the logistic model, abs_dlog in the tree: two views of the same signal). These are the trustworthy drivers. Perishability, smaller markets and higher district deprivation are positive in the logistic model only. A depth-2 tree can use just three features, so its silence on them is not evidence against them.

Initial policy takeaways (SDG 2.c). These are hedged: they rest on four test weeks and modest signal.

  1. Where to look. Concentrate weekly monitoring on perishables, especially those whose price already moved this week. Branded and packaged goods almost never move abnormally.
  2. At most a triage aid, not a trigger. With test AUC around 0.7 and precision around 5%, most alerts will be false. A model score can rank where an inspector looks first; it should not trigger stock releases or penalties by itself.
  3. A simple rule already captures most of the signal. "Watch perishables" matched the logistic model on the test weeks. Any data system PBS or a province builds should be benchmarked against that rule before it is trusted.
  4. Publish every week. 40% of labelled rows have no previous consecutive week, because the published weekly archive has gaps. The moves we miss are exactly those with no visible recent history. Consistent publication of the weekly annexure is a cheap way to improve early warning.
  5. The threshold is a policy choice. Whether alerts are worth issuing depends on how many false alarms one missed shock is worth. Below about 20, silence is cheaper. That judgment, and whether to weight misses in deprived districts more, belongs to decision-makers.

10. Limitations and Milestone 04 roadmap

Limitations.

  • Sample size. Only 205 positives over 15 labelled weeks. The test set has 54 positives in four weeks, so every test number has a wide interval.
  • Intervals and the archive. The bootstrap treats rows as independent, but rows in the same week share shocks, so the true uncertainty is larger; the per-week check uses only four weeks. Several diagnostics (costs, alert budget, error breakdown) reuse the same test predictions. PBS archive gaps cost 40% of rows their recent history and limit how many weeks can be labelled.
  • Signal. Class imbalance and modest signal. KNN is unsuitable for this data. Two tuned settings sit at the edge of their grids (C = 0.01, K = 201), which suggests the data favour very smooth models.
  • Label. The 90th-percentile threshold is a convention. A stricter or looser cut-off would change the base rate and the results (§7).
  • Context variables. City-level variables (population, deprivation from 2019-20) are constant within a city. Islamabad and Rawalpindi function as one market but are scored separately.
  • Scope. 17 urban SPI cities only, so possible place-based bias in who gets missed (§8.3). The Milestone 02 limitations on coverage, deprivation data and measurement still apply.

Milestone 04 plan.

  • Advanced models: random forest and gradient boosting, compared against today's two benchmarks and three baselines under the same forward-chaining CV, purge and cost analysis.
  • Fairness audit (H4): recall, FN rate and calibration compared across province, population tercile and deprivation tercile, with a hypothesis test, and including whether the single-quote data-quality flag drives errors.
  • Thresholds and ethics: group-aware thresholds that equalise miss rates, and their cost. An ethical evaluation of who bears the false negatives and the false alarms.
  • Week 14 new data: the design already committed in Milestones 01-02. First, score the frozen model on the post-cutoff holdout weeks without refitting. Then retrain on the earlier part of the new data and test on a later, unseen part.
